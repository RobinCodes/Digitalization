The Story

On the impulse to record

There is a moment every student of mathematics knows — the moment a proof finally closes, when the last line follows inexorably from the first, and the whole argument stands before you like a cathedral: complete, load-bearing, impossible to improve. The instinct that follows is almost physical: write it down. Not because you fear forgetting — though you will forget — but because the thing deserves to exist somewhere outside your own head, in a form that will outlast the urgency of the afternoon.

This archive began as exactly that impulse, repeated across years and subjects. A note here, a derivation there. A proof that took three weeks to understand compressed into two careful pages. A theorem whose statement I had seen a hundred times but whose proof I had never truly seen — until I sat down and wrote it myself, line by line, in my own notation.

"Mathematics is the art of giving the same name to different things." — Henri Poincaré

What surprised me, over time, was how much the act of writing changed the act of understanding. To write a proof is to interrogate it. Every elision you allow yourself in reading becomes a gap you must close in writing. The argument that seemed clear at a glance reveals, on paper, that it was only familiar — which is a very different thing.

On the beauty of the thing

I did not always believe that mathematics was beautiful. Early on it seemed merely difficult — a discipline of rules and exceptions, of techniques to memorise and procedures to execute. It was only later, when the techniques became fluent enough to recede into the background, that the actual structure of mathematics became visible, and that structure was startling.

"The mathematician does not study pure mathematics because it is useful; he studies it because he delights in it, and he delights in it because it is beautiful." — Henri Poincaré

There is the beauty of pure surprise: that the sum of the reciprocals of the perfect squares converges to exactly π²/6, a fact that connects the integers to the circle with no obvious reason why it should. There is the beauty of deep unity: that the same group-theoretic structures govern the symmetries of a crystal, the solutions of a polynomial, and the fundamental particles of matter. There is the beauty of compression: that a single line — Maxwell's equations, the Euler–Lagrange equation, the Riemann hypothesis — can contain more content than volumes of prose.

G. H. Hardy wrote that there is no permanent place in mathematics for ugly mathematics, and while one may argue with his examples, the underlying claim resonates: beauty and correctness in mathematics are not independent. An ugly proof is usually not a final proof. It still contains the shape of a better argument, waiting to be found.

"It is not knowledge, but the act of learning — not possession, but the act of getting there — which grants the greatest enjoyment." — Carl Friedrich Gauss

On the method of LaTeX

Every document in this archive is written in LaTeX — not out of convention or inertia, but because the medium shapes the thought. To typeset a formula properly, you must understand its structure. The distinction between $f(x)$ and $f \colon X \to Y$ is not cosmetic; it is a distinction in meaning, and LaTeX enforces it. The discipline of the source file is a discipline of precision.

There is also something right about the permanence of plain text. A LaTeX source file written in 1990 compiles today, unchanged. The mathematics does not decay. The theorems that Euler, Gauss, and Riemann established remain as true now as the day they were proved — and a plain-text file encoding them has a lifespan that no proprietary format can match.

"Pure mathematics is, in its way, the poetry of logical ideas." — Albert Einstein

This archive, then, is not a database. It is a collection of documents — each one the record of an encounter with an idea, typeset with care, stored in a structure simple enough to understand in an afternoon and robust enough to last decades. The goal was never comprehensiveness. It was honesty: to write down only what I actually understand, in enough detail that I will understand it again when I return.

On what an archive is for

Hermann Weyl described mathematics as the science of the infinite — the discipline through which the human mind, finite and mortal, reaches toward structures that transcend it. There is something quietly extraordinary about that. Every proof in this archive is an attempt, however small, to participate in that reaching.

"In mathematics you don't understand things. You just get used to them." — John von Neumann

Von Neumann's remark is usually read as deflationary — a warning against false confidence. But I have come to read it differently. To get used to something, in mathematics, means to have built so many correct intuitions around it that it no longer surprises you. That process of habituation is itself a form of understanding, earned slowly, through repetition and return. The archive is the record of that process. Not a monument to conclusions, but a map of a journey still in progress.

If someone else reads these notes and finds them useful, that is a gift I did not anticipate. But the primary reader was always a future version of myself — one who has forgotten the details, but not the conviction that the details are worth knowing.

"The essence of mathematics lies in its freedom." — Georg Cantor
